# Expert Survey Report on the Quality of DRHL-Generated Access-Control Patches

## 1. Study objective and scope

The expert study was conducted to assess the practical quality of successful access-control patches generated by DRHL. The assessment covered three distinct dimensions: Access-Control Correctness, Functionality Preservation, and Code Quality. The study did not evaluate all patches generated by DRHL. It evaluated 16 selected successful patches, one from each benchmark application, and its conclusions are therefore limited to those 16 patches [1, 2].

DRHL successfully repaired 48 vulnerabilities in the full experiment, whereas the expert study assessed 16 of those repairs. The survey consequently provides direct evidence for one third of the successful repairs and should not be presented as a complete manual assessment of all 48 repairs [1].

## 2. Evidence sources and reporting boundary

This report is based on two supplied sources:

1. Section 5.6 and Table 18 of the DRHL manuscript, which describe participant recruitment, case-selection criteria, survey procedure, aggregate ratings, and inter-rater agreement [1].
2. The questionnaire platform's aggregate report, which presents the 16 cases, the before-and-after code shown to participants, and the per-case rating distributions for the three quality dimensions [2].

The questionnaire report contains aggregate counts rather than participant-level response records. It does not contain a demographic questionnaire, individual qualifications, free-text comments, timestamps, or a rater-by-case response matrix. Accordingly, this report does not infer details that are absent from the supplied materials.

## 3. Participants and qualifications

The study involved 18 graduate researchers, including master's and doctoral students actively engaged in security research. Participants were selected for research experience in at least one of three relevant areas: software security, web security, or access-control analysis [1]. These criteria provide a domain-relevant basis for asking participants to assess authorization logic and source-code patches.

The supplied materials do not report the number of master's versus doctoral participants, their years of research or development experience, institutional affiliations, prior experience with the four evaluated programming-language families, or whether their eligibility was verified through a formal screening instrument. The term expert in this report therefore refers to the domain-relevant graduate research background stated in the manuscript, rather than to a quantified professional certification or minimum number of years of experience.

## 4. Recruitment

Participants were recruited through direct invitation based on their relevant research experience [1]. This is a criterion-based, purposive recruitment strategy: the study sought raters with security knowledge rather than a random sample of software developers or students.

The supplied materials do not state how many people were invited, how many declined or failed to complete the questionnaire, the resulting response rate, whether participation was compensated, or whether recruitment was conducted within one institution or across several institutions. The available evidence therefore supports reporting the achieved sample and eligibility basis, but not a recruitment response rate or population-level representativeness claim.

The aggregate questionnaire tables contain 18 ratings for every case-dimension pair. Thus, the available report shows no item-level missing ratings among the 18 included participants [2].

## 5. Case selection

The study selected 16 successful repair cases, with one case drawn from each of the 16 benchmark applications. When an application contained multiple successful repairs, the authors selected the case considered most representative of the application's access-control scenario and having the greatest security impact [1]. This strategy ensured application-level coverage and exposed participants to PHP, Java/JSP, Python, and Go patches. It also covered both vertical and horizontal privilege-escalation scenarios.

The selection was not random. Because only successful repairs were eligible and the final case from each application was chosen using representativeness and security-impact judgments, the sample is suitable for examining the quality of selected successful patches but not for estimating the quality of every generated patch. The supplied materials also do not provide a formal scoring rubric for representativeness or security impact, so the exact selection decision cannot be independently reproduced from the report alone.

## 6. Survey procedure and quality dimensions

For each case, participants received the vulnerability description, the expected authorization behavior, the vulnerable source code, and the corresponding patched source code. They evaluated the patch separately on the following dimensions [1, 2]:

- Access-Control Correctness: whether the patch prevents the unauthorized operation and enforces the expected authorization requirement.
- Functionality Preservation: whether legitimate functionality remains available to authorized users.
- Code Quality: whether the patch is consistent with the original codebase and remains readable and maintainable.

Each dimension was rated on a five-point Likert scale: Strongly Disagree, Disagree, Neutral, Agree, and Strongly Agree. Thus, the instrument did not use one combined quality score. Each participant provided three separate ratings for each of 16 cases, producing 48 ratings per participant, 288 ratings per dimension, and 864 dimension-level ratings in total [1, 2].

The supplied materials do not report case-order randomization, blinding, time limits, training examples, attention checks, or free-text justification. Participants were shown the expected authorization behavior, which gave them a common reference for judging correctness but also means that the evaluation was not blind to the intended policy.

## 9. Inter-rater agreement

The manuscript reports Krippendorff's alpha with ordinal distance separately for each dimension [1]:

| Quality dimension | Krippendorff's alpha |
| --- | ---: |
| Access-Control Correctness | 0.016 |
| Functionality Preservation | 0.010 |
| Code Quality | 0.041 |

These values indicate limited agreement on the exact Likert category assigned by individual participants. Therefore, the study should not claim strong inter-rater consensus. The appropriate interpretation is that the marginal rating distributions are predominantly positive, while raters often differed between adjacent response categories, particularly Agree and Strongly Agree.

High positive-rating percentages and low agreement coefficients address different questions. The former show that most ratings fell on the positive side of the scale; the latter show that participants did not consistently choose the same exact ordinal category. Both results should be reported together.

The questionnaire PDF contains only aggregate counts and does not provide the rater-by-case response matrix required to independently recompute Krippendorff's alpha. The agreement coefficients in this report therefore reproduce the values reported in the manuscript rather than an independent recalculation.

## 10. Data-consistency check

One discrepancy exists between the two supplied sources. Summing the 16 per-case questionnaire tables yields 228 Agree and 47 Strongly Agree ratings for Functionality Preservation [2]. Table 18 of the manuscript reports 229 Agree and 46 Strongly Agree [1]. The difference corresponds to one rating shifted between two adjacent positive categories. It does not affect the number of positive ratings, the 95.49% positive rate for Functionality Preservation, or the overall 94.68% positive rate. However, the two cells and the corresponding Overall row should be reconciled against the original participant-level export before submission.

All other dimension-level counts in Table 18 agree with the questionnaire's per-case tables.

The manuscript also contains an unrelated summary inconsistency: the abstract and Section 5.6 report an overall positive-rating rate of 94.68%, whereas the contribution summary in the Introduction reports an 87.15% approval rate [1]. The questionnaire data support 94.68% because 818 of 864 ratings are Agree or Strongly Agree. The 87.15% value should therefore be corrected or its alternative computation explicitly explained.

## 11. Interpretation and limitations

The survey supports four bounded findings about the 16 assessed patches. First, participants gave predominantly positive ratings for Access-Control Correctness. Second, Functionality Preservation was also rated positively, suggesting that the assessed patches generally retained intended authorized behavior in the code examples presented to the raters. Third, Code Quality remained positive overall but attracted more neutral ratings than the other dimensions. Fourth, the near-zero agreement coefficients show that the raters did not exhibit strong exact-category agreement, even though their ratings were concentrated on the positive side of the scale.

These findings are subject to the following limitations:

- The study evaluated 16 selected successful repairs, not all 48 successful repairs and not unsuccessful repair attempts.
- The selection was purposive rather than random and used qualitative notions of representativeness and security impact.
- Participant qualifications are reported only at a broad graduate-research level; detailed expertise and demographic breakdowns are unavailable.
- The recruitment denominator and response rate are unavailable.
- The survey report contains aggregate rather than participant-level data, preventing independent agreement recalculation or analysis of participant-specific effects.
- The supplied materials do not describe blinding, case-order randomization, formal calibration, or qualitative comments.
- Agreement on exact Likert categories was limited, so the positive distribution should not be described as strong rater consensus.

## 12. Conclusion

Within the 16 selected successful repairs, 94.68% of the 864 dimension-level ratings were Agree or Strongly Agree. Access-Control Correctness received 97.22% positive ratings, Functionality Preservation received 95.49%, and Code Quality received 91.32%. These results provide positive evidence for the practical quality of the specific patches assessed in the questionnaire. They do not establish the quality of every patch generated by DRHL, and the low Krippendorff's alpha values require the results to be described as predominantly positive rating distributions rather than strong inter-rater agreement.
